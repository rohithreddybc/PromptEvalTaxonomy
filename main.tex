\begin{abstract}
Prompt engineering evaluation lacks a shared conceptual frame. Two
papers can both report improvements on the same prompting technique
using methods, metrics, and scopes that are internally consistent yet
mutually incomparable -- not because either study is poorly designed,
but because the field has not agreed on what should be measured or
how. We trace this to two unresolved tensions from measurement
theory: construct validity vs. measurement reliability, and
reference-anchored vs. judgment-anchored evaluation. Existing
evaluation choices project onto these tensions in identifiable,
predictable ways.

This paper develops a seven-dimensional taxonomy of prompt
engineering evaluation frameworks organized around those tensions,
covering evaluation method, task scope, metric type, automation
level, evaluation scope, robustness, and reproducibility. We applied
it to a corpus of 153 papers from a PRISMA 2020 systematic review of
six academic databases (an initial 80-paper set expanded through four
supplementary search rounds covering LLM-judge quality, benchmark
contamination, multi-prompt sensitivity, agentic and multilingual
evaluation, prompt-injection safety, CoT faithfulness,
foundation-model technical reports, RAG evaluation, bias and fairness,
calibration, and clinical/legal domain evaluation) and used it to
derive co-occurrence patterns, recurring evaluation
\textit{archetypes}, and a catalog of failure modes where a paper's
evaluation configuration does not support its contribution claims.

Three patterns emerge from the data. The dominant configuration --
benchmark-based, task-specific, fully automated evaluation -- sits in
the high-reliability, narrow-validity region of the design space and
works well for tasks with correct answers, but under-specifies the
construct for open-ended generation. The LLM-as-judge cluster
addresses this gap at the cost of introducing judge-model reliability
concerns the field has not managed with a shared calibration
protocol. Robustness and contamination-resistant evaluation appear as
reported properties in only a minority of papers, suggesting these
checks are not yet standard.

The taxonomy is not a new evaluation method. It is a shared
vocabulary for making evaluation design choices, their tradeoffs, and
their interactions visible to both the researcher designing a study
and the reader interpreting its results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Within roughly four years, large language models (LLMs) have moved
from academic novelties into foundational components of production
software systems. That period has also generated substantial research
on prompt engineering (PE), the practice of crafting natural language
inputs to elicit reliable, useful model outputs without modifying
weights~\cite{liu2021pretrain, schulhoff2024prompt}. Chain-of-thought
prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, automated prompt
optimization, and dozens of related techniques have been proposed,
evaluated, and deployed across tasks as varied as arithmetic reasoning
and clinical decision support~\cite{wei2022chain, lewis2020retrieval, sahoo2024systematic}.

To see why evaluation practice matters, consider a concrete scenario.
A researcher proposes a chain-of-thought variant for medical
differential diagnosis. Study~A evaluates it on MedQA using
exact-match accuracy against a fixed answer key (Evaluation Method =
Benchmark, Metric = Task-specific, Automation = Fully automated).
Study~B evaluates the same technique using GPT-4 as a clinical
scoring judge on a different question set (Evaluation Method =
LLM-as-judge, Metric = LLM-judge, Automation = Fully automated).
Both studies report improvements over their respective baselines.

A clinician or systems architect choosing between the two techniques
cannot combine these results. Study~A's accuracy gain does not
establish clinical utility: exact-match against a multiple-choice
answer key does not capture the reasoning quality or safety properties
that clinical deployment requires. Study~B's GPT-4 judge may or may
not be calibrated to the judgment standard that a domain expert would
apply; without a calibration experiment, the score is uninterpretable
relative to clinical ground truth. This is not a failure of rigor
inside either study. It is the consequence of an evaluation ecosystem
without a shared vocabulary for describing what each study measured,
which tradeoffs each configuration accepts, and under what conditions
results transfer across labs. Providing that vocabulary -- a
seven-dimensional taxonomy grounded in measurement theory -- is the
primary contribution of this paper.

The scenario is not unusual. The same prompting strategy can be
assessed using benchmark accuracy on one model, human preference
ratings on another, LLM-judge scoring on a third, and pass@$k$ on
code generation tasks for a fourth. Each evaluation is internally
consistent. None is directly comparable to the others.
Researchers deciding which technique to apply to a new problem have
no principled basis for interpreting results across studies, because
the evaluation frameworks underlying those results are organized
along different, unstated, and mutually incompatible
assumptions~\cite{chang2023survey, guo2023evaluating}.

This has practical consequences. Prompt engineering claims
underpin design decisions in commercial systems, clinical tools, and
automated software pipelines. When those claims rest on evaluation
setups that cannot be compared or reproduced across labs, the
inferences they support are difficult to evaluate. The field needs a
shared vocabulary for describing evaluation frameworks - not a single
mandated protocol, but a structure that makes the relevant choices
visible so researchers can reason about them explicitly.

\subsection*{Contribution}

This paper provides that taxonomy and develops the conceptual
structure beneath it. We argue that the configuration space of
prompt engineering evaluation is organized by two underlying tensions
from measurement theory: \textit{construct validity vs. measurement
reliability}~\cite{cronbach1955construct, messick1995validity,
jacobs2021measurement}, and \textit{reference-anchored vs.
judgment-anchored evaluation}. The patterns observed in the corpus
map onto that space in coherent, predictable ways rather than
appearing as an arbitrary scatter of methodological choices.

The taxonomy covers seven dimensions: evaluation method, task scope,
metric type, automation level, evaluation scope, robustness and
prompt sensitivity, and reproducibility and contamination resistance.
Each dimension projects primarily onto one of the two underlying
tensions. The projections, and the empirical co-occurrence patterns
they predict, are documented in Sections~\ref{sec:taxonomy}
and~\ref{sec:comparison}.

\subsection*{Research Questions}
\label{subsec:rqs}
This survey is organized around four research questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] \textit{(Taxonomy.)} What dimensions are required to
classify the evaluation framework of any prompt-engineering study in
a way that exposes its measurement-theoretic trade-offs? Addressed
in Sections~\ref{sec:taxonomy} and~\ref{subsec:tensions}.

\item[RQ2] \textit{(Distribution.)} What are the empirical
distributions of evaluation choices across the 153-paper corpus, and
which combinations co-occur often enough to constitute recurring
\textit{archetypes}? Addressed in
Sections~\ref{subsec:d1dist}--\ref{subsec:cooccurrence} and
\ref{subsec:archetypes}.

\item[RQ3] \textit{(Failure modes.)} Where does the taxonomy expose
specific points at which an evaluation configuration does not support
the paper's contribution claim? Addressed in
Section~\ref{subsec:failuremodes}.

\item[RQ4] \textit{(Tensions and gaps.)} What structural tensions
organize the corpus, and what gaps in evaluation practice does the
taxonomy reveal that prior surveys do not centrally address?
Addressed in Sections~\ref{subsec:tension_scalability}--\ref{subsec:tension_reproducibility}
and the future-direction agenda of Section~\ref{subsec:agenda}.
\end{description}

We apply the taxonomy to a corpus of 153 papers from a PRISMA 2020
systematic literature review of six databases. An initial
primary-search set of 80 papers was expanded across four
supplementary search rounds
(80 $\rightarrow$ 100 $\rightarrow$ 111 $\rightarrow$ 146 $\rightarrow$ 153)
addressing areas underrepresented in the primary results: LLM-judge
quality and bias, benchmark contamination detection, multi-prompt
sensitivity, agentic and multilingual evaluation, prompt-injection
safety, chain-of-thought faithfulness, retrieval-augmented generation
evaluation, calibration, and clinical/legal domain evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}). The data reveal three patterns
within the inclusion criteria documented in
Section~\ref{subsec:inclusion}. The most common configuration --
benchmark-based, task-specific, fully automated evaluation --
occupies the high-reliability, narrow-validity region of the design
space and works well for tasks with ground-truth answers but
under-specifies the construct on open-ended generation tasks. The
LLM-as-judge cluster addresses this under-specification at the cost
of introducing judge-model reliability concerns the corpus does not
yet manage with a shared calibration protocol. Robustness and
contamination-resistant evaluation appear in only a small minority of
papers; the pattern suggests a structural reporting gap under our
inclusion criteria, the boundaries of which are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison}.

The taxonomy's contribution is not a new evaluation method but a
shared conceptual frame for reasoning about evaluation design. It
makes the tradeoffs between validity and reliability visible at the
level of individual coding choices, identifies recurring evaluation
\textit{archetypes} (Section~\ref{subsec:archetypes}) that describe
how those choices co-vary in the literature, and exposes the specific
points at which current practice trades validity for reliability or
vice versa.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Three intersecting bodies of prior work bear directly on this survey:
prompt engineering methods, LLM evaluation frameworks, and taxonomy
and survey papers in NLP. We characterize what each stream has
contributed and where each falls short.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering Methods and Techniques}

Interest in systematic prompt design grew once researchers confirmed that
input phrasing alone could meaningfully shift model outputs~\cite{brown2020gpt3}. Early
work established the foundational paradigms. Few-shot prompting supplies the model
with worked examples alongside the task description, rather than instructions alone~\cite{brown2020gpt3}.
Chain-of-thought prompting extends this approach by embedding intermediate reasoning
steps within those examples. That addition substantially improved
accuracy on arithmetic and commonsense tasks~\cite{wei2022chain}. Zero-shot
CoT achieves a similar effect without any examples, relying on
a single instructional cue~\cite{kojima2022large}. Later contributions include
self-consistency decoding~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, tree-of-thought
prompting~\cite{yao2023tree}, and retrieval-augmented generation, which
fetches relevant external documents at inference time~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}.

More recent surveys have tried to bring order to this space. Li
et al.~\cite{li2025autoprompt} frame automated prompt optimization as a
maximization problem over discrete, continuous, and hybrid prompt spaces.
Ramnath et al.~\cite{ramnath2025apo} organize the same territory around
a five-part framework. What neither addresses is the evaluation question:
once you have a prompting method, how do you know it actually worked,
and by what standard?~\cite{schulhoff2024prompt,sahoo2024systematic}

\subsection{Evaluation Frameworks for Large Language Models}

LLM evaluation developed along its own trajectory. GLUE~\cite{wang2018glue} and
SuperGLUE~\cite{wang2019superglue} established widely used baselines for natural language
understanding. BIG-Bench~\cite{srivastava2022bigbench} targeted harder
reasoning tasks that earlier models could not handle. MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring}
assessed multitask knowledge at scale across dozens of subject areas. MT-Bench and
AlpacaEval~\cite{zheng2023judging, dubois2024alpacaeval} redirected focus
to open-ended instruction following, using LLM-based judges rather than
fixed answer keys. Ni et al.~\cite{ni2025benchmarks} reviewed 283 benchmarks and found recurring problems across the board: data contamination, cultural bias, and static test sets that models can eventually memorize.

Human evaluation retains the highest credibility for open-ended
tasks, but its cost and latency push most studies toward automated
alternatives such as BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu},
ROUGE~\cite{lin2004rouge}, or an LLM judge. Gu et
al.~\cite{gu2025llmjudge} document the systematic biases of
LLM-based judges - positional bias, verbosity preference,
inconsistency across prompting strategies - and Feng et
al.~\cite{feng2025sage} showed that even top-performing models fail
to maintain consistent preferences in roughly a quarter of difficult
pairwise cases. The available evaluation tools are numerous; what is
missing is a shared structure for comparing across them.

\subsection{Surveys and Taxonomies in Prompt Engineering and LLM Evaluation}

Across the survey literature, prompting methods and evaluation practice
have been treated as separate topics with no systematic bridge between
them. Schulhoff
et al.~\cite{schulhoff2024prompt} produced the most thorough inventory of
prompting techniques to date, cataloguing 58 text-only prompting techniques, establishing a standardized
vocabulary, and contributing a meta-analysis of the literature, but the work is a catalog of
techniques rather than a framework for evaluating them. Sahoo
et al.~\cite{sahoo2024systematic} and Vatsal and
Dubey~\cite{vatsal2024survey} take similar approaches, organizing methods
by application domain and NLP task type respectively. On the evaluation
side, Chang et al.~\cite{chang2023survey} organize LLM evaluation around
what, where, and how to evaluate. Ni et al.~\cite{ni2025benchmarks} sort 283 benchmarks into a three-tier taxonomy. Mohammadi
et al.~\cite{mohammadi2025agents} propose a two-dimensional taxonomy for
agent evaluation.

The closely related surveys reviewed here do not centrally address
a question that this work foregrounds: for a researcher evaluating a
specific prompting technique, which evaluation framework should they
use, and why? Answering that requires classifying evaluation
frameworks along dimensions that are meaningful for PE work
specifically. That classification is what this survey provides.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Large Language Models and the Role of Prompting}

Large language models are transformer-based neural networks trained on
web-scale text through self-supervised prediction. At sufficient scale,
such training yields models capable of handling a broad range of tasks
without task-specific retraining~\cite{brown2020gpt3, liu2021pretrain}. Practitioners interact
with these capabilities through prompts: natural language inputs that condition what the
model generates.

A key complication is that surface-level phrasing choices produce
nontrivial output differences. Sclar et al.\ showed this
is not a marginal effect: formatting choices as small as delimiter type and
instruction position shift accuracy by measurable margins across multiple
models~\cite{sclar2023formatting}. Zhou et al.\ showed that automatically
generated prompts sometimes outperform those written by experienced
humans~\cite{zhou2022ape}. Prompt engineering developed in response to
this observation. It is the practice of designing inputs to get reliable, useful
outputs without modifying the model~\cite{sahoo2024systematic}. The field
now has its own benchmarks, optimization algorithms, and competing
taxonomies~\cite{schulhoff2024prompt}.

\subsection{The Prompt Engineering Design Space}

The prompting design space spans a spectrum from entirely manual to fully
automated. Zero-shot prompting supplies the model with a task description
and nothing else. Few-shot
prompting adds worked examples, which orients the model toward the expected
reasoning style and output format~\cite{brown2020gpt3}. Chain-of-thought
prompting includes intermediate reasoning steps in those examples.
This one addition substantially improves accuracy on arithmetic, symbolic, and
commonsense tasks~\cite{wei2022chain}. Retrieval-augmented generation
connects the prompt to external documents fetched at inference
time~\cite{lewis2020retrieval, ragsurvey2023}.

At the automated end, prompt optimization treats the prompt as the variable
to search over. Textual gradient descent uses model error feedback to
revise wording iteratively~\cite{pryzant2023apo}. Reinforcement learning
frames prompt tokens as a discrete policy optimized against task
reward~\cite{rlprompt2022}. LLM-based optimizers use one model to generate
and evaluate candidates for a second~\cite{zhou2022ape}.

The question that cuts across all of these methods is the same: how do
you know it worked? That depends on which evaluation framework you apply,
and the field currently has no shared way of classifying those frameworks. That is
what this survey provides.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric Families}

Four metric families appear with regularity across the prompt engineering
evaluation literature. They form the basis of Dimension~3 in the taxonomy, and
understanding their differences matters directly for the analysis in
Sections~\ref{sec:comparison} and~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\textbf{Reference-based metrics} score model output by measuring surface
overlap against a human-written gold reference. BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} counts
$n$-gram precision; ROUGE~\cite{lin2004rouge} applies recall-oriented overlap
for summarization tasks. Both are fast and reproducible, but their alignment with human
judgment is known to be weak on tasks that admit multiple valid
phrasings~\cite{liu2023geval, repairing2022}. A perfectly correct answer expressed
in different words will still receive a low score.

\textbf{Model-based metrics} rely on learned semantic representations rather
than word overlap. They handle paraphrase more gracefully and tend to align more closely
with human ratings. They still require a reference text, however, and they
carry forward whatever gaps and biases that reference contains~\cite{chang2023survey}.

\textbf{LLM-as-judge metrics} delegate scoring to a second language model,
eliminating the need for a gold reference. Liu et al.\ showed that G-Eval, using
GPT-4 with structured rubric prompts, achieves stronger alignment with
human judgment than BLEU and ROUGE on
summarization and dialogue tasks~\cite{liu2023geval}.
The trade-off is that LLM judges import their own systematic biases:
positional preference, a tendency to favor longer responses, and
self-enhancement bias in which a model rates its own outputs higher than
those produced by other models~\cite{zheng2023judging, gu2025llmjudge}.

\textbf{Task-specific metrics} are purpose-built and generally do not transfer.
Pass@$k$ tests whether code passes unit tests within $k$ attempts. Exact
match verifies factual retrieval. RAGAS measures faithfulness and context
recall for retrieval-augmented pipelines~\cite{rageval2024}. These metrics are
often the most meaningful available for their intended task. But a paper using pass@$k$
and a paper using RAGAS share no common ground, which is a core reason why evaluation results across the PE field remain so difficult to compare.

The taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} organizes these four
metric families across seven dimensions, anchored to two
measurement-theory concepts that recur throughout the rest of the
paper.

\subsection{Validity and Reliability in Evaluation}
\label{subsec:validityreliability}

The reliability of a measurement is the consistency with which it
produces the same value when applied repeatedly under nominally
identical conditions. The construct validity of a measurement is the
degree to which it captures the underlying property it is intended to
capture - the degree to which the score \emph{means what we want it to
mean}~\cite{cronbach1955construct, messick1995validity}.

The two are not equivalent and frequently pull against each other. A
fully scripted exact-match comparison against a fixed answer key has
high reliability: the same input produces the same score on every
run. Its construct validity for a property like ``answer quality'' is
narrow, however, because it gives the same score to a brilliant
restatement and a mechanical reproduction. A panel of human experts
scoring open-ended outputs can pursue construct validity at whatever
level the deployment context demands, but inter-rater agreement is
rarely perfect and the panel cannot be replicated cheaply or
quickly~\cite{chang2023survey}. Recent work translates this distinction
into ML and NLP evaluation directly~\cite{jacobs2021measurement,
subramonian2023takeoneforteam, liao2023designing}: a high score on a
benchmark whose construct does not match the deployment task is not
evidence about the deployment property; it is evidence about the
benchmark.

Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} uses validity and reliability as the
spine of the dimensional decomposition. We adopt the terminology in
its measurement-theory sense throughout: a metric or pipeline is
\textit{reliable} when it produces consistent scores under repetition,
and a measurement has \textit{construct validity} when its scores are
meaningful evidence about the property of interest in the deployment
context.

\subsection{Formal Definitions}
\label{subsec:definitions}

The following five definitions formalize the conceptual vocabulary
used throughout the rest of the paper. Each definition is tight by
design; the operationalizations and their empirical projections are
developed in Sections~\ref{sec:taxonomy} and~\ref{sec:comparison}.

\begin{definition}[Prompt Engineering Evaluation Framework]
A prompt engineering evaluation framework $\mathcal{F}$ is a tuple
$(m,\, t,\, q,\, a,\, s,\, r,\, p)$ where each component
corresponds to a choice along one of the seven dimensions defined
in Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}: $m$ (evaluation method, D1), $t$
(task scope, D2), $q$ (metric type, D3), $a$ (automation level,
D4), $s$ (evaluation scope, D5), $r$ (robustness reporting, D6),
and $p$ (reproducibility properties, D7). Any complete instantiation
of the tuple defines a valid evaluation configuration; the corpus
in Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is analyzed as a distribution over
such configurations.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Construct Validity]
An evaluation configuration $\mathcal{F}$ has construct validity for
a target property $\pi$ to the degree that high scores under
$\mathcal{F}$ constitute genuine evidence that a model or prompting
technique achieves $\pi$ in the intended deployment context.
Construct validity is partial and degree-valued, not binary: a
benchmark measuring arithmetic accuracy has high construct validity
for arithmetic tasks and substantially lower construct validity for
claims about open-ended clinical reasoning~\cite{cronbach1955construct,
messick1995validity, jacobs2021measurement}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Measurement Reliability]
An evaluation configuration $\mathcal{F}$ is reliable if repeated
applications of $\mathcal{F}$ to the same model--prompt pair produce
scores with small variance under nominally identical conditions.
\textit{Within-run reliability} (fixed software, fixed model
snapshot) and \textit{across-time reliability} (across API revisions,
judge-model updates, or benchmark re-releases) are distinct
sub-properties; high within-run reliability does not imply
across-time reliability~\cite{subramonian2023takeoneforteam}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Reference-Anchored Evaluation]
An evaluation configuration is \textit{reference-anchored} if scoring
is determined by comparison to a stored gold answer -- an exact-match
key, a reference translation, or a passing test suite -- without
invoking a separate judge. Reference-anchored configurations have
high within-run reliability but fail when no single correct answer
exists, when the reference set is contaminated by training data, or
when the reference under-determines acceptable outputs.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Judgment-Anchored Evaluation]
An evaluation configuration is \textit{judgment-anchored} if scoring
is delegated to an evaluator -- a human annotator, an expert panel,
or an LLM judge -- whose output is not pre-determined by a stored
reference. Judgment-anchored configurations can pursue construct
validity beyond what any fixed reference set supports, but they
introduce evaluator-specific biases and require explicit calibration
before scores carry cross-study interpretability~\cite{gu2025llmjudge,
feng2025sage}.
\end{definition}

\section{A Taxonomy of Prompt Engineering Evaluation Frameworks}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

\subsection{Two Underlying Tensions in Evaluation Design}
\label{subsec:tensions}

A useful taxonomy of evaluation frameworks should do more than catalog the
choices researchers make. It should explain why those choices cluster the
way they do. We argue that the configuration space of prompt engineering
evaluation is structured by two largely orthogonal tensions, each
inherited from the broader measurement literature and each visible in the
distribution of practice across our corpus
(Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).

The first tension is between \textit{construct validity} and
\textit{measurement reliability}. Construct validity asks whether a measurement
captures the property it is intended to capture; reliability asks whether
the same measurement, applied repeatedly, yields the same
result~\cite{cronbach1955construct, messick1995validity}. The two
properties are not equivalent and frequently pull in opposite directions.
A pass@$k$ score against a fixed test suite is highly reliable
- a second run on the same model and the same suite will return the same
number - but its construct validity for ``code quality'' is narrow, since
unit tests do not capture readability, maintainability, or security. A
panel of expert clinicians scoring open-ended medical answers can pursue
construct validity at the level the deployment context demands, but the
panel cannot be replicated cheaply or at scale, and inter-rater agreement
is rarely perfect~\cite{chang2023survey}. Recent work in machine learning
evaluation explicitly translates this validity-reliability framing into
the ML setting~\cite{jacobs2021measurement, subramonian2023takeoneforteam,
liao2023designing}, and we adopt it here as the spine of the taxonomy.

The second tension is between \textit{reference-anchored} and
\textit{judgment-anchored} evaluation. Reference-anchored evaluation
compares a model's output to a stored gold answer (or a structured
property of the answer, such as a passing test). Judgment-anchored
evaluation delegates the decision to an evaluator - a human reviewer, a
panel of experts, or a second language model acting as judge. The two
modes have different failure characteristics. Reference-anchored
evaluation fails when no plausible gold reference exists, when references
under-determine acceptable outputs (translation, summarization), or when
the reference set is contaminated by training data. Judgment-anchored
evaluation fails when judges import biases that the protocol does not
detect~\cite{zheng2023judging, gu2025llmjudge, feng2025sage}, when
inter-judge calibration is absent, or when the judge is itself a
language model whose internal state cannot be audited.

These two tensions are largely orthogonal. A single study can be
reference-anchored and reliability-favoring (benchmark accuracy),
judgment-anchored and validity-favoring (expert panel review), or any
combination in between. The seven dimensions introduced in
Sections~\ref{subsec:d1}--\ref{subsec:d7} project onto these axes in
specific, identifiable ways, and the configurations that papers
actually adopt cluster in regions of the space that the
validity/reliability frame predicts. Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} positions
the dimensions on the two-axis space; Section~\ref{sec:comparison} then
locates 153 papers within it.

\textit{What the frame explains.} The validity/reliability framing is
not a re-labeling exercise; it is intended to do explanatory work.
Three behaviors of the literature follow directly from the two
tensions and would be left unexplained by a flat taxonomy:

(a) \emph{Why D1 and D3 are entangled.} A paper that anchors
scoring to a stored gold answer (D1 = Benchmark) almost always
adopts the metric the benchmark prescribes (D3 = Task-specific or
Reference-based). The entanglement is not a coding artifact; it
reflects the fact that choosing a judgment locus largely determines
the metric family. The frame predicts the joint distribution
observed in Section~\ref{subsec:cooccurrence}.

(b) \emph{Why benchmark-automation is the modal configuration.}
The reliability axis is the only axis on which automation pays
near-zero validity cost \emph{when the task admits a single correct
answer}. For closed-form reasoning, arithmetic, and code-execution
tasks, the construct is well-approximated by the reference, so the
high-reliability end of the axis is also the high-construct-fidelity
end. For open-ended generation tasks the same configuration pays
significant construct validity for the same reliability. The frame
predicts that Archetype A (benchmark-automation) will dominate
the corpus and will fail in a specific direction
(Section~\ref{subsec:tension_scalability}).

(c) \emph{Why LLM-judge adoption fragments.} Moving from
reference-anchored to judgment-anchored scoring (D1 = LLM-as-judge)
preserves throughput but introduces a new reliability concern at the
judge model itself. Without calibration evidence (a sub-property of
D3) and judge-version pinning (D7), the within-paper reliability
the configuration retains does not transfer to across-paper
comparability. The frame predicts both the empirical concentration
of LLM-judge papers in Archetype B and the fragmentation documented
in Section~\ref{subsec:tension_reference}.

The frame is therefore not just a way of organizing the dimensions;
it is a low-parameter account of why the literature distributes
itself the way it does.

\subsection{From Five Dimensions to Seven}
\label{subsec:fivetoseven}

An earlier formulation of this taxonomy used five dimensions:
evaluation method, task scope, metric type, automation level, and
evaluation scope. Application to the corpus revealed two limitations
of the five-dimensional version. First, the five dimensions
captured \emph{procedural} choices but did not name two properties
that the literature itself treats as first-class evaluation concerns:
robustness to prompt rephrasing~\cite{sclar2023formatting,
promptsensitivity2025, butterfly2024} and reproducibility under judge
drift, API change, and benchmark contamination~\cite{ni2025benchmarks,
feng2025sage}. Second, the five dimensions did not place automation
level in its proper relation to reliability. Automation is not an
independent property of evaluation; it is a particular operationalization
of the reliability axis, and treating it as a peer to evaluation method
or metric type misrepresents the conceptual structure.

The current version of the taxonomy retains the five original dimensions
but adds two: \textit{Dimension~6 (Robustness)} and
\textit{Dimension~7 (Reproducibility and Contamination Resistance)}. It
also reframes \textit{Dimension~4 (Automation Level)} explicitly as a
sub-axis of reliability rather than an independent procedural choice.
The resulting seven-dimensional structure is intended to make the two
underlying tensions traceable through every coding decision.

As formalized in Definition~1, a prompt engineering evaluation
framework is fully specified by a choice along each of the seven
dimensions D1\textendash D7. The dimensions are conceptually
distinct -- each can vary independently of the others -- but they are
not statistically independent in practice.
The co-occurrence patterns documented in
Section~\ref{subsec:cooccurrence} are a finding of this survey, not a
flaw in the dimensional decomposition. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}
summarizes all seven dimensions with subcategories, the axis each
dimension primarily projects onto, and a representative example.

\subsection{Dimension 1: Evaluation Method}
\label{subsec:d1}

How is a prompting technique assessed? Method is primarily a projection
onto the judgment-locus axis: reference-anchored at the benchmark and
ablation end, judgment-anchored at the human, LLM-judge, and user-study
end. Five subcategories appear in the corpus.

\textbf{Benchmark-based evaluation} applies a prompting technique to
a curated dataset with known correct answers. MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring},
BIG-Bench~\cite{srivastava2022bigbench}, and HellaSwag are representative examples.
Results are reproducible and comparable across studies, which explains why this
remains the default method across most of the literature. The main weakness is
saturation: as model capabilities improve, benchmarks lose discriminative
power, and data contamination is a well-documented concern~\cite{ni2025benchmarks}.

\textbf{Human evaluation} asks annotators to judge output quality through
absolute scoring or pairwise preference. It is generally the most credible option
for open-ended tasks, but it is also resource-intensive, slow to execute, difficult to
replicate, and rarely accompanied by strong inter-annotator agreement~\cite{chang2023survey}.

\textbf{LLM-as-judge evaluation} replaces human annotators with a second language model
in the scoring role. MT-Bench and AlpacaEval are prominent examples of this
approach~\cite{zheng2023judging, dubois2024alpacaeval}. It scales efficiently and
handles open-ended outputs well, but the judge carries its own systematic biases
that multiple independent studies have now documented~\cite{gu2025llmjudge}.

\textbf{Ablation study} isolates the contribution of individual components
by selectively removing or modifying them. This approach is well suited to
understanding what drives a technique's performance, though it is not designed
for cross-technique comparison.

\textbf{User study} collects evidence from real users performing authentic
tasks, measuring outcomes such as task completion rates, satisfaction, or efficiency. User studies are rare
in the NLP literature but are important whenever the deployment context
involves end users rather than benchmark leaderboards~\cite{chang2023survey}.

\subsection{Dimension 2: Task Scope}
\label{subsec:d2}

What type of task is the evaluation targeting? Task scope acts as a
\textit{moderator} on the validity-reliability tradeoff: the same metric
choice yields very different validity properties depending on whether
the task admits a single gold answer (arithmetic reasoning, exact
factual retrieval) or only a distribution of acceptable
answers (open-ended generation, clinical decision support). Scope is
arguably the most fragmented dimension across the literature. A
technique that performs strongly on arithmetic reasoning may behave
quite differently on clinical named entity recognition, yet many papers
treat their chosen task as broadly representative without testing
whether their findings hold elsewhere.

Our review identifies eight primary task categories that together span
the range of tasks observed in the corpus (plus a cross-category
\textbf{Multi-task} label for papers spanning multiple domains; see
below).

\textbf{Reasoning tasks} include arithmetic, symbolic, commonsense, and
multi-step logical reasoning. This is the most heavily studied category,
largely because chain-of-thought prompting~\cite{wei2022chain} was initially developed
for reasoning problems and most early follow-on evaluations naturally adopted that framing.

\textbf{Natural language generation tasks} cover summarization, dialogue,
translation, and open-ended writing. Since these tasks admit no single correct
answer, they require human or LLM-based evaluation rather than
exact-match scoring.

\textbf{Code generation tasks} assess whether a prompted model produces
functionally correct code, typically measured via pass@$k$ against a test
suite~\cite{scoT2023, promptalchemy2025}.

\textbf{Question answering tasks} range from closed-book factual retrieval to
open-book reading comprehension, and are conventionally scored with exact match or F1.

\textbf{Clinical and domain-specific tasks} include medical QA, clinical
note processing, and legal reasoning. Accuracy requirements are considerably higher in these settings,
evaluation routinely requires domain experts, and available benchmarks are
smaller and more specialized~\cite{clinicalnlp2024, medicalcot2025}.

\textbf{Multilingual tasks} evaluate techniques across languages and
tend to surface performance gaps that monolingual evaluations
would otherwise conceal~\cite{vatsal2024survey}.

\textbf{Agentic and tool-use tasks} evaluate prompted models acting
as autonomous decision-makers across multi-step workflows that
invoke external tools, search APIs, code interpreters, or
sub-agents~\cite{yao2024taubench}. Evaluation is
trajectory-oriented (success rate over multi-step traces) rather than
single-output, and requires sandboxes that simulate the tool API
deterministically.

\textbf{Multimodal tasks} extend prompted evaluation to inputs and
outputs beyond text -- typically vision-language pairs (visual
question answering, image captioning, OCR-grounded reasoning) and
increasingly audio and video~\cite{fu2023mme}. Scoring requires
modality-appropriate references (image-grounded BLEU/CIDEr,
visual-grounding accuracy) or judgment-anchored alternatives.

Papers evaluated across multiple task domains without specializing in
any single category are coded as \textbf{Multi-task}; this is a
cross-category label rather than a peer primary category, and appears
as a ninth entry in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} for completeness.

The corpus distribution across these eight primary task categories
(and the Multi-task label) is reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:d2dist}. Agentic and Multimodal entered the
taxonomy during expansion rounds 2--3
(Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}) when those papers were
re-admitted; they are present in lower numbers than the first six
categories because they are recent. Future revisions of the
taxonomy may further subdivide these as the population grows
(Section~\ref{subsec:multimodal}).

\subsection{Dimension 3: Metric Type}
\label{subsec:d3}

What is being measured, and by what means? Metric type is the most direct
projection of the reference-anchored / judgment-anchored axis introduced
in Section~\ref{subsec:tensions}: reference-based and task-specific
metrics anchor scoring to a stored gold answer, while LLM-as-judge
and (some) model-based metrics anchor scoring to an evaluator's
judgment. Section~\ref{sec:background} introduced the four metric
families; we apply them directly here: reference-based
(BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE~\cite{lin2004rouge}), model-based
(BERTScore), LLM-as-judge (G-Eval~\cite{liu2023geval}), and task-specific
(pass@$k$, exact match, RAGAS~\cite{rageval2024}). Calibration evidence
- whether the metric has been validated against a target judgment, e.g.
human annotators on the specific task - is a sub-property of D3 that
becomes methodologically central for judgment-anchored metrics.

Metric choice is not independent of the other dimensions, and making
these interdependencies explicit is a central contribution of this
classification. Reference-based metrics only function when a gold
reference exists; they are poorly suited for open-ended generation.
Task-specific metrics do not transfer outside their native task family.
LLM-as-judge metrics import the systematic biases of the underlying
judge model and require explicit calibration before scores carry construct
meaning~\cite{liu2023geval, gu2025llmjudge}. Classifying papers by
metric type surfaces systematic patterns in how the field matches, and
sometimes mismatches, its metrics to its tasks.

\subsection{Dimension 4: Automation Level}
\label{subsec:d4}

How much human involvement does the evaluation require? Unlike the
preceding dimensions, automation level is not a peer of method, task,
or metric. It is best understood as a \textit{sub-axis of reliability}:
fully automated pipelines maximize across-run consistency at the cost of
whatever construct validity human judgment would have contributed, while
fully manual pipelines invert that tradeoff. We retain D4 in the
taxonomy because the corpus codes it independently and the
human-in-loop / hybrid distinction is practically meaningful, but its
conceptual weight is lower than D1, D3, or D5.

The four subcategories are: \textit{fully manual} (all judgment in
human hands), \textit{human-in-loop} (selective routing of borderline
cases to human reviewers~\cite{ouyang2022training}), \textit{hybrid}
(automated scoring for the bulk of items plus uniform human validation
on a held-out sample), and \textit{fully automated} (no human in any
stage of the loop). The human-in-loop and hybrid categories are not
interchangeable: human-in-loop applies judgment selectively on hard
cases while hybrid applies it uniformly across a fixed sample.

\subsection{Dimension 5: Evaluation Scope}
\label{subsec:d5}

What is being compared? Scope projects onto a third axis that is
largely orthogonal to the validity-reliability frame: it determines how
far a paper's conclusions can generalize beyond the specific setup in
which results were obtained. A maximally reliable, maximally valid
single-model evaluation still yields a narrow claim; a noisier
cross-model evaluation may yield a broader one.

\textbf{Single-model, single-dataset} is a common baseline
configuration: one technique, one model, one benchmark. Results are
internally clean but generalizability is entirely unverified. The
modal configuration in the corpus is in fact cross-model
(Section~\ref{subsec:d5dist}); single-model-single-dataset is the
narrowest end of the scope axis.

\textbf{Cross-model evaluation} applies the same technique to multiple
model families, directly testing whether performance is specific to one architecture
or holds more broadly.

\textbf{Cross-dataset evaluation} holds the task type fixed while varying
the benchmark. This probes robustness within a given task family.

\textbf{Cross-domain evaluation} applies a technique across qualitatively
different task types. It represents the most demanding scope and remains the least common in
individual papers, though survey works such as Sahoo
et al.~\cite{sahoo2024systematic} cover it implicitly by design.

\textbf{Cross-lingual evaluation} tests performance across languages. As multilingual
prompt engineering research matures, this scope is beginning to appear more
frequently in the literature~\cite{vatsal2024survey}.

Scope matters because a substantial share of strong claims in the PE literature
rest on single-model, single-dataset evidence. A technique that performs
well on GPT-4 for arithmetic may not transfer to smaller open-source models or
different task types. Without explicit scope classification, those limits stay invisible to readers.

\subsection{Dimension 6: Robustness and Prompt Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:d6}

Does the evaluation establish that the reported performance is stable
under semantically equivalent rephrasings of the prompt? Robustness is a
property of the construct being measured, not of the measurement
instrument, and it therefore projects onto the validity axis.
A technique that performs well under one prompt phrasing but degrades
sharply under paraphrase is not, in any meaningful sense, the technique
the paper claims to evaluate; the construct ``effectiveness of
chain-of-thought prompting'' includes the assumption that
\textit{semantically equivalent} chain-of-thought instructions yield
\textit{statistically equivalent} performance.

The empirical case for treating robustness as a first-class dimension is
already in the literature. Sclar et al.~\cite{sclar2023formatting} showed
that delimiter and formatting changes shift accuracy by margins large
enough to reverse leaderboard rankings. Mizrahi et al.'s butterfly-effect
study~\cite{butterfly2024} documents similar variance under paraphrase.
Razavi et al.'s PromptSET~\cite{promptsensitivity2025} provides a
sensitivity benchmark explicitly. Bellibaltu et al.'s Judge Sensitivity
Score~\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense} extends sensitivity reporting to
LLM-as-judge pipelines themselves, defining JSS over 500 hand-validated
paraphrase pairs across four evaluation tasks (factuality, coherence,
relevance, preference) and reporting it for 13 judge models. They
find that even GPT-4o flips its coherence rating on 8.5\% of paraphrase
pairs and that smaller instruction-tuned judges flip on more than 60\%,
and crucially that model scale is not a reliable proxy for stability.
Yet the corpus we surveyed treats
sensitivity as a finding rather than a dimension: papers rarely report
performance bounds across paraphrases, and reviewers rarely require
them.

The four robustness subcategories used in our coding are: \textit{not
reported} (no sensitivity analysis), \textit{single-prompt} (one prompt
phrasing, no variance information), \textit{multi-prompt aggregate}
(performance averaged across paraphrases without per-prompt breakdown),
and \textit{sensitivity-bounded} (variance or stability bounds reported
explicitly). The four-level coding lets us distinguish papers that
ignore sensitivity from those that report it without quantifying it,
and from the smaller set that establish bounds.

\subsection{Dimension 7: Reproducibility and Contamination Resistance}
\label{subsec:d7}

Can a third party reproduce the reported results, and are those
results stable under realistic infrastructure drift? Reproducibility
projects onto the reliability axis, but it captures aspects that D4
(automation level) does not. A fully automated evaluation pipeline
that depends on a proprietary judge model accessed via a versioned API
is reliable in the within-run sense (the same call returns the same
score) and unreliable in the across-time sense (the judge can change
silently between API revisions, as documented for GPT-4
snapshots~\cite{feng2025sage}). Contamination is a further reproducibility
threat: a benchmark whose answers have leaked into pretraining produces
inflated scores that subsequent runs cannot correct~\cite{ni2025benchmarks,
raji2021ai}.

The four reproducibility subcategories used in our coding are:
\textit{artifact-incomplete} (no released code, prompts, or seeds),
\textit{artifact-released} (code, prompts, and seeds available),
\textit{contamination-checked} (the study explicitly verifies that
test items did not appear in training data, e.g.\ via held-out
construction~\cite{wildbench2024} or contamination probes), and
\textit{judge-version-pinned} (where applicable: the API version,
snapshot date, or local model checkpoint of any LLM-as-judge component
is reported and recoverable). The categories are not mutually
exclusive; a paper can satisfy multiple. We code each as a binary
property and report combined patterns in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}.

\subsection{Why These Seven Dimensions and Not Others}
\label{subsec:whyseven}

A natural objection to a seven-dimensional taxonomy is that it could
plausibly be six or eight. We anticipate three such objections and
address them directly.

\textit{Why not include calibration as a separate dimension?} LLM-judge
calibration - the alignment of a judge's scores with a target judgment,
typically human - is increasingly treated as a precondition for valid
LLM-as-judge results~\cite{liu2023geval, alternativeannotator2025}.
We classify calibration as a sub-property of D3 (Metric Type) rather
than a top-level dimension because calibration is meaningful only for
metrics whose construct relationship is non-obvious. Reference-based
metrics on closed tasks have transparent constructs; calibration in
that setting is degenerate. Promoting calibration to D8 would force
trivial codings on most of the corpus.

\textit{Why not include uncertainty quantification as a separate
dimension?} Methods such as semantic entropy~\cite{semanticentropy2024}
or pairwise variance reporting are increasingly used to express
confidence in evaluation outputs. We treat uncertainty as a sub-property
of D6 (Robustness): variance under input perturbations is one
operationalization of uncertainty about the construct being measured.
A separate uncertainty dimension would overlap substantially with the
robustness coding.

\textit{Why not include deployment realism (interactive use, latency
constraints, multi-turn dynamics) as a separate dimension?} Deployment
realism is partially captured by D1 subcategory \textit{user study},
by D2 subcategories \textit{clinical and domain-specific} and
\textit{Agentic}, and by D5 subcategory \textit{cross-domain}. The
recently expanded Agentic subcategory in D2 (added in expansion rounds
2--3 with the entry of $\tau$-bench, AgentBench, AgentBoard, and
related work) is the current proxy for the deployment-realism corner
of the design space. We do not promote deployment realism to a
separate dimension because the agentic population, though now
present, is still small and overlaps substantially with the existing
D2 / D5 coding; if agentic and multi-turn evaluation continues to
grow as a primary PE deployment context, a future revision of the
taxonomy could promote it.

The seven dimensions are therefore not the only conceivable
decomposition, but they correspond to choices the corpus actually makes,
project cleanly onto the two underlying tensions, and avoid trivial
codings on the bulk of the literature.

\section{Applying the Taxonomy: Worked Examples and Design Guidance}
\label{sec:taxonomy_application}

The taxonomy becomes useful only when researchers can apply it, both
to classify existing work and to structure their own evaluation designs.
This section demonstrates both uses. Subsection~\ref{subsec:classify}
works through two papers already in the corpus to show how classification
proceeds dimension by dimension. Subsection~\ref{subsec:design} walks a

Subsection~\ref{subsec:quickref} distills the guidance into a single

\subsection{Classifying Existing Papers}
\label{subsec:classify}

To illustrate the classification process, consider two widely cited papers
that represent distinct evaluation configurations.

\textbf{Paper: Wei et al., ``Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning
in Large Language Models''~\cite{wei2022chain}.}
D1\,=\,Benchmark (performance is measured on established arithmetic
and commonsense benchmarks such as GSM8K and SVAMP, using standardized
test sets with gold answers).
D2\,=\,Reasoning (all tasks - arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense -
are closed-form reasoning problems).
D3\,=\,Task-specific (exact-match accuracy is used, which is the
natural metric for tasks with a single correct answer).
D4\,=\,Automated (no human judgment is involved; evaluation is
fully script-based against reference answers).
D5\,=\,Cross-model (results are reported across multiple model scales,
including PaLM 540B, GPT-3, and LaMDA).
D6\,=\,Single-prompt (one chain-of-thought template per benchmark; no
paraphrase variance reported).
D7\,=\,Artifact-released (prompts and exemplar sets disclosed); not
contamination-checked at the time of publication.

\textbf{Paper: Zheng et al., ``Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and
Chatbot Arena''~\cite{zheng2023judging}.}
D1\,=\,LLM-as-judge (GPT-4 is used as the primary evaluator, scoring
model responses on a 1-10 scale).
D2\,=\,Multi-task (MT-Bench spans eight categories including writing,
roleplay, math, and coding, with no single task domain dominating).
D3\,=\,LLM-as-judge (the scoring rubric is operationalized entirely
through GPT-4 judgments rather than reference-based or human-assigned
metrics; calibration is reported against human pairwise preferences
on a held-out subset).
D4\,=\,Automated (the MT-Bench component that the taxonomy classifies
relies on automated GPT-4 scoring; Chatbot Arena's human preferences
are an adjunct, not the primary evaluation channel).
D5\,=\,Cross-model (the paper compares 28 open-source and proprietary
models simultaneously).
D6\,=\,Multi-prompt aggregate (positional and verbosity bias probes
are reported, but per-prompt sensitivity bounds are not).
D7\,=\,Judge-version-pinned (GPT-4 snapshot is identified) but not
contamination-checked.

The compact notation D1\,=\,Benchmark / D2\,=\,Reasoning /
D3\,=\,Task-specific / D4\,=\,Automated / D5\,=\,Cross-model /
D6\,=\,Single-prompt / D7\,=\,Artifact-released can be embedded
directly in a citing paper's methodology section to describe its
evaluation configuration relative to this taxonomy.

\subsection{Designing a New Evaluation}
\label{subsec:design}

Consider a researcher evaluating a new retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
prompting technique for clinical question answering. Before any data
collection begins, the taxonomy requires seven explicit design decisions.

\textit{D1 - Which evaluation method?} Benchmark evaluation assumes a
curated test set with gold reference answers. For clinical QA, such sets
are scarce, domain-constrained, and often legally sensitive. Human
evaluation or a hybrid approach is more defensible here: domain experts
can assess factual correctness and safety in ways that static benchmarks
cannot. A researcher choosing D1\,=\,Benchmark for clinical QA should
document why the available benchmark adequately covers the clinical
accuracy requirements.

\textit{D2 - Which task scope?} The task is unambiguously clinical,
placing this work in a sparsely represented region of the corpus
(see Section~\ref{sec:comparison}). Choosing D2\,=\,Clinical makes
that positioning explicit and signals that existing evaluation
instruments from general NLP may not transfer without adaptation.

\textit{D3 - Which metric?} No gold reference exists for open-ended
clinical responses, making reference-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE)
structurally inadequate. Model-based factual precision metrics such as
FActScore~\cite{factscore2023} are defensible if the knowledge source is
well-defined. An LLM-as-judge configuration (D3\,=\,LLM-judge) is an
alternative, but requires calibration against clinician judgments on a
held-out set before the scores carry clinical interpretability.

\textit{D4 - What automation level?} Clinical annotation requires expert
time, which is expensive. Full automation (D4\,=\,Automated) reduces cost
but risks systematic errors that clinicians would catch. Human-in-loop
(D4\,=\,Human-in-loop) is more appropriate for a first evaluation in a
domain where no calibrated automated metric yet exists.

\textit{D5 - What evaluation scope?} A single-model result
(D5\,=\,Single-model) in clinical QA provides the weakest possible
generalizability evidence. Cross-model evaluation (D5\,=\,Cross-model) is
required before the technique can be recommended for deployment across
clinical systems using different underlying models.

\textit{D6 - What robustness reporting?} Clinical RAG systems are
particularly exposed to prompt sensitivity because retrieved-context
phrasing varies across patient records. A
D6\,=\,Sensitivity-bounded design - reporting variance across
paraphrased clinical queries on a held-out evaluation set - is
defensible; D6\,=\,Single-prompt should be flagged as a known
generalizability risk in the limitations section.

\textit{D7 - What reproducibility commitments?} For clinical
evaluation, D7 should aim at \textit{judge-version-pinned} (if any LLM
component is used) and \textit{contamination-checked} (verifying that
the test set is not subset of any training corpus the underlying
model has seen). Because medical benchmarks are smaller and easier to
contaminate, D7=\,Artifact-incomplete is rarely defensible in this
domain.

This exercise surfaces seven design decisions that are typically
implicit and unreported. Using the taxonomy to make them explicit
before beginning evaluation reduces the risk of post-hoc
rationalization and makes the resulting study directly comparable to
prior work.

\section{Methodology: Systematic Literature Review}
\label{sec:methodology}
% (duplicate sec:methodology label removed; sec:methodology above is canonical)

The 153-paper corpus was constructed through a PRISMA 2020 systematic
literature review~\cite{page2021prisma}. The review consists of one
primary search round (Sections~\ref{subsec:search}--\ref{subsec:screening},
Figure~\ref{fig:prisma}) followed by four targeted expansion rounds
(Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}). All five rounds apply the PRISMA
2020 reporting conventions~\cite{page2021prisma}: stated databases,
documented search strategy with dates, explicit inclusion / exclusion
criteria (Section~\ref{subsec:inclusion}), dual-reviewer screening
with reported agreement (Section~\ref{subsec:screening}, $\kappa$ =
0.81 over a 200-record dual-screened sample), per-round identified /
screened / excluded / included counts (Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}),
and dual coding of every included paper (Section~\ref{subsec:coding_reliability},
$\kappa$ = 0.76--0.92 by dimension). The primary round and the four
expansion rounds differ only in the breadth of their queries: the
primary round used full database sweeps with broad PE-evaluation
search strings, while the expansion rounds used the same databases
with narrower topical refinements (Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds},
``Target areas''). The expansion rounds are therefore documented as
``additional records identified from other sources / supplementary
searches'' within the PRISMA 2020 flow~\cite{page2021prisma}, which
explicitly allows for such rounds.

\subsection{Search Strategy}
\label{subsec:search}

the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Semantic Scholar, Scopus,
and Web of Science. The first four are the principal preprint and

work. Scopus and Web of Science extend coverage to a wider range of journal sources

publication-year cutoff, though nearly all relevant papers in the

\subsection{Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria}
\label{subsec:inclusion}

\textbf{Included:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Papers that propose, evaluate, or compare one or more
prompt engineering techniques using a stated evaluation framework
\item Papers that propose new evaluation frameworks, benchmarks,
or metrics relevant to assessing prompted LLM outputs
\item Survey and taxonomy papers covering prompt engineering or
LLM evaluation
\item Conference papers, journal articles, and arXiv preprints
with at least one of: peer review, 10+ citations, or publication
at a recognized NLP/AI venue (ACL, EMNLP, NeurIPS, ICLR, IEEE,
ACM)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Excluded:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Papers that use prompting as a method but do not report
or discuss evaluation of the prompting approach itself
\item Papers with no accessible full text
\item Non-English papers
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Re-admittance criterion for expansion rounds 2--3.}
The initial PRISMA round excluded papers whose \emph{topic} was prompt
injection, jailbreaking, or narrow clinical/legal application. In
expansion rounds 2 and 3 (Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}), this
exclusion was refined: papers in these areas were re-admitted when
their \emph{evaluation methodology} (not their attack or clinical
content) is what the taxonomy classifies -- e.g., prompt-injection
\emph{benchmarks} contribute evaluation infrastructure even though
their topic is an attack family, and clinical PE benchmarks such as
MIRAGE / MedRAG~\cite{xiong2024medrag} contribute
multi-prompt and reference-anchored evaluation evidence even if the
underlying study is domain-applied. We document the re-admittance
choice explicitly because it changed the corpus boundary between
rounds. Papers admitted under this refinement are listed and flagged
in the Electronic Supplement (Appendix~C).

This distinction is practically consequential: many papers use prompting as a tool
(e.g., ``we prompted GPT-4 to extract entities'') without
reporting or analyzing the evaluation of the prompting approach
in any systematic way. Those papers were excluded because they do not contribute
meaningfully to understanding evaluation frameworks.

\textbf{On mixing paper types.} The corpus deliberately includes four
overlapping paper categories: (1) prompt engineering technique
papers, (2) benchmark and dataset papers, (3) LLM-as-judge and
metric-quality papers, and (4) prompt engineering and LLM evaluation
surveys. A reviewer may reasonably ask whether mixing these categories
into one analytical corpus is methodologically defensible. Our
position is that all four categories \emph{participate in PE
evaluation infrastructure}: technique papers consume benchmarks and
metrics, benchmark and metric papers shape what techniques can be
evaluated, and surveys recapitulate practice and influence its
direction. The unifying inclusion principle is participation in
evaluation infrastructure, not paper genre. To allow auditable
claims, however, we report all major distributions in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} both in aggregate and split by paper
category, and we do not aggregate across categories where the
category is itself the moderator of interest (for instance,
LLM-as-judge configurations are reported separately for the
LLM-as-judge methodology subgroup).

\subsection{Screening Process}
\label{subsec:screening}

Database queries returned 2,847 unique records after
deduplication. Two reviewers independently screened all titles and
abstracts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, working
separately at each phase. Disagreements at title/abstract screening
were resolved through discussion (percent agreement 94.1\%, $\kappa$
= 0.81 over a 200-record dual-screened sample); unresolved cases were
referred to a third reviewer, as specified in PRISMA 2020
Item~8~\cite{page2021prisma}.

Title and abstract screening advanced 412 papers to full-text
review. Of these, 283 were excluded: 181 used prompting as a tool
without evaluating the prompting approach itself, 64 were
domain-specific applied papers outside scope, 24 had no accessible
full text, and 14 were non-English. The remaining 129 papers passed
full-text screening. Among papers without peer review, retention was
conditioned on venue: only papers at recognized NLP/AI venues (ACL,
EMNLP, NeurIPS, ICLR, IEEE, or ACM proceedings) or indexed journals
were retained, reducing the set to 58 papers. The 22 papers recovered
through citation searching then brought the initial corpus to
\textbf{80 papers}. Four supplementary expansion rounds then added
papers in areas the original search string was likely to undercount
(Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}). The same inclusion and exclusion
criteria were applied at each round, with two refinements documented

For each paper in the final corpus, we extracted the following

\item Evaluation method (Dimension~1)
\item Task scope (Dimension~2)
\item Metric type(s) used (Dimension~3)
\item Automation level of the evaluation (Dimension~4)
\item Evaluation scope (Dimension~5)

\subsection{Coding Reliability}
\label{subsec:coding_reliability}

Two independent coders extracted dimensional codes for the corpus.
The primary coder coded all papers; the second coder independently
re-coded a stratified random subsample of at least 25\% of
the final corpus (${\geq}39$ papers), stratified across paper categories, without
access to the primary coder's labels. Disagreements were resolved
through discussion; cases that could not be resolved were referred
to a third reviewer following PRISMA 2020 Item~8~\cite{page2021prisma}.

Inter-rater agreement is reported as Cohen's $\kappa$ per dimension
in Table~\ref{tab:kappa}. The dimensions split into two reliability
regimes. Objectively verifiable dimensions (D1 evaluation method, D2
task scope, D3 metric type, D5 evaluation scope) achieved $\kappa$
between 0.88 and 0.92 on the dual-coded sample, all in the
``almost perfect'' tier of the Landis and Koch scale. Interpretively
coded dimensions (D4 automation level, D6 robustness, D7
reproducibility) achieved $\kappa$ between 0.76 and 0.84, in the
``substantial'' tier. The lower $\kappa$ on D6 and D7 reflects
genuine ambiguity at category boundaries rather than disagreement on
facts: papers that report a single sentence acknowledging
sensitivity without quantifying it sit between D6=Not reported and
D6=Single-prompt, and papers that release code but not data sit
between D7=Artifact-released and D7=Artifact-incomplete. We report what we measured rather than collapsing to a single
corpus-level $\kappa$, since the heterogeneous reliability profile is
itself part of the finding: some evaluation properties are codable
from descriptions, others require interpretive judgment that an
evaluation reporting standard could substantially reduce.

Ambiguity-resolution rules are documented in the dataset repository
linked above. The most consequential rules were: (a) for D3 papers
using multiple metric families, the \textit{primary} metric (the one
used for the headline claim) was recorded; (b) for D4 papers
reporting a small spot-check of an otherwise automated pipeline, the
paper was coded as hybrid only if the spot-check result conditioned
a reported decision (e.g.\ a filter, a threshold), not if it was
descriptive; (c) for D6, papers that reported sensitivity \emph{only
as a finding} (sensitivity studies themselves) without using
sensitivity as part of the headline evaluation pipeline were coded as
\textit{multi-prompt aggregate}, not sensitivity-bounded; (d) for
D7, contamination-checked required an explicit verification step,
not a generic statement that the test set differed from training.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}

To check whether the headline distributional claims in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} are stable under coding uncertainty,
we report a sensitivity analysis. We perturbed 10\% and 20\% of
dimensional codes uniformly at random,
re-derived the headline percentages (benchmark dominance, automation
dominance, single-model evaluation share, clinical and multilingual
representation), and report the resulting bounds in
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}. Headline claims that move by less than
two percentage points under 20\% perturbation are reported as stable;
claims that move more are flagged with explicit uncertainty bounds in
the relevant Section~\ref{sec:comparison} subsections. All headline claims are robust to both perturbation levels: no claim
moves by more than 3.4 percentage points under 20\% perturbation.
The task-specific metric share shows the largest shift (3.4~pp under
20\% perturbation), which is borderline-stable relative to the 2-pp
stability threshold; this claim should be read with that caveat.
All other claims move by less than 2~pp, confirming stability.

\subsection{Validity Threats}

Three limitations constrain what this review can reliably claim, and
each has direct implications for how readers should interpret the
distributional findings in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}.

\textit{Search coverage.} The search strings rely on controlled
vocabulary in title, abstract, and keyword fields and may miss papers
that address the same underlying concepts under alternative
terminology. Citation searching recovered 22 additional papers in the
primary round (bringing the initial corpus to 80); four subsequent
expansion rounds then added 20, 11, 35, and 7 papers, bringing the
final corpus to 153 (Section~\ref{subsec:screening}).
Completeness cannot be guaranteed. We are explicit throughout the
paper that absences in the corpus (notably the relative scarcity of
clinical and multilingual primary targets) are statements about
\emph{this corpus under these inclusion criteria}, not about the
field as a whole. Broader prompting literature on those domains
exists~\cite{clinicalnlp2024, medicalcot2025, vatsal2024survey}, and
our finding is that this literature is not yet incorporated into the
systematic evaluation practice indexed by our search.

\textit{Quality filter.} The retention rule excludes papers with
fewer than 10 citations that were not published at a recognized
venue, which could underrepresent very recent 2025--2026 work. We
partially addressed this by retaining 2025 and 2026 papers that met
the venue criterion regardless of citation count, but the
distribution of recent methodological developments in the LLM-as-judge
and dynamic-benchmark subfields may still be undercounted.

\textit{Coding judgment at category boundaries.} Several dimensions
require interpretation when a paper does not explicitly characterize
its evaluation along the corresponding axis. The dual-coding and
inter-rater statistics in Section~\ref{subsec:coding_reliability}
quantify the resulting uncertainty per dimension; the sensitivity
analysis in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} establishes how much
that uncertainty propagates to the headline claims.

\section{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comparison}

Mapping the corpus against the seven-dimensional taxonomy serves
three purposes. First, it tests whether the taxonomy is expressive
enough to characterize the range of evaluation choices observed in
the literature within our inclusion criteria. Second, it derives
co-occurrence patterns, recurring \textit{evaluation archetypes},
and a catalog of failure modes that go beyond per-dimension frequency
counts. Third, it generates the empirical foundation for the
synthesis in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\paragraph{PE-core papers vs.\ evaluation infrastructure.} The
corpus deliberately contains two overlapping populations: \emph{PE-core
papers} (prompt-engineering technique papers, sensitivity / code
prompting studies, and PE-specific evaluation surveys -- 76 papers
total in the categories of Table~\ref{tab:comparison}) and
\emph{evaluation infrastructure papers} (benchmarks, datasets,
LLM-judge frameworks, and metric-quality studies -- 77 papers
total). Together they constitute the 153-paper corpus. The mixed
inclusion is justified in Section~\ref{subsec:inclusion}: technique
papers consume infrastructure and infrastructure papers shape what
techniques can be evaluated, so the two populations participate in
shared PE evaluation practice. We retain the mixed corpus for the
aggregate distributional findings below, but where a claim is
sensitive to the split we report it separately by paper category
(D-distributions in Section~\ref{subsec:d6dist} are an example).
Readers who want a PE-technique-only view can filter
the Electronic Supplement (Appendix~C) on the per-paper category column.

We organize the analysis as follows. Sections~\ref{subsec:d1dist}
through~\ref{subsec:d7dist} report per-dimension distributions, both
in aggregate and split by paper category (technique, benchmark,
LLM-judge / metric, survey) so that aggregate claims can be audited
against the categories that drive them. Section~\ref{subsec:cooccurrence}
reports the dimension co-occurrence matrix and identifies the small
number of configurations that account for the bulk of the corpus.
Section~\ref{subsec:archetypes} names the recurring evaluation
archetypes that emerge from those configurations and locates each on
the validity-reliability frame from Section~\ref{subsec:tensions}.
Section~\ref{subsec:failuremodes} then walks through three to five
worked examples in which a paper's evaluation configuration does not
support its contribution claim - cases where the taxonomy makes a
genuine methodological mismatch visible.

five most common configurations across the seven dimensions, their
share of the corpus, and the underlying tension each foregrounds.

comprehensive 153-paper mapping is in

\subsection{Distribution across Evaluation Method (Dimension 1)}
\label{subsec:d1dist}

Benchmark-based evaluation accounts for 107 of the 153 papers in the
corpus (69.9\%), establishing it as the default across every subgroup
in the review. Representative examples include Wei et al.~\cite{wei2022chain} on
chain-of-thought prompting and Hendrycks et al.~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring}
on multitask language understanding. Both studies report accuracy against
fixed-answer datasets, and this pattern repeats consistently throughout
the reasoning and benchmark subgroups. The foundation-model technical
reports for GPT-4~\cite{achiam2023gpt4},
LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023llama}, Llama
2~\cite{touvron2023llama2}, and GPT-4o~\cite{hurst2024gpt4o}
similarly anchor on this benchmark-automated configuration; their
release-time evaluations rely almost entirely on fixed-answer
benchmarks for the headline capability claims. Benchmark results are
reproducible, comparable across labs, and inexpensive to compute at
scale. What they cannot capture is output quality for open-ended tasks
where no single correct answer exists.

LLM-as-judge evaluation appears in 32 papers (20.9\%), concentrated
almost entirely in the open-ended generation and instruction-following
subgroups. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023judging} introduced the MT-Bench
framework for this purpose and were candid about its limitations alongside
its strengths. Gu et al.~\cite{gu2025llmjudge} and Feng et al.~\cite{feng2025sage}
have since documented the reliability concerns more systematically. The
corpus also includes the fine-tuned scalable judges of Zhu et
al.~\cite{zhu2023judgelm} (JudgeLM), the panel-of-judges approach by Verga
et al.~\cite{verga2024replacing}, and the agent-debate meta-evaluation
framework of Chern et al.~\cite{chern2024scaleeval} (ScaleEval). Recent
work by Ye et al.~\cite{ye2024justice} and Wataoka et
al.~\cite{wataoka2024selfpreference} quantifies the bias profile of LLM
judges directly. LLM judges address a genuine need: many tasks have no
gold reference to score against. But adoption has clearly outpaced the
research into when and how much to
trust them.

Human evaluation appears in only 16 papers (10.5\%), and ablation
studies in 2 (1.3\%). No paper in the corpus employs a user study as
its primary method. The human-evaluation cluster combines work that
either (i) uses human ratings as the primary signal, as in Jang and
Lukasiewicz's consistency analysis of
ChatGPT~\cite{jang2023consistency} and the Bubeck et al.\ ``Sparks of
AGI'' capability probe~\cite{bubeck2023sparks}, (ii) uses humans to
audit safety-critical behavior, as in the medical red-teaming of
Chang et al.~\cite{chang2025redteamingmed} and the human-LLM
calibration-gap study of Steyvers et
al.~\cite{steyvers2024calibgap}, or (iii) compares humans against LLMs
on cognitive tasks, as in the Theory-of-Mind battery of Strachan et
al.~\cite{strachan2024theoryofmind}. The LLMBar meta-evaluation
benchmark by Zeng et al.~\cite{zeng2024llmbar} provides a
complementary measurement of how well LLM judges substitute for human
raters on instruction-following. Human evaluation remains the acknowledged
standard for tasks involving subjective quality
judgments~\cite{chang2023survey}, but its share in this corpus is
small. Read against the overall distribution, the pattern is
consistent with a tradeoff in which the corpus systematically favors
configurations that maximize reliability and scale -- benchmark and
LLM-judge pipelines -- over configurations that prioritize the
construct-validity guarantees human evaluation provides. We return
to this tradeoff in Section~\ref{subsec:tension_scalability}.

\subsection{Distribution across Task Scope (Dimension 2)}
\label{subsec:d2dist}

Multi-task or cross-task evaluations account for 89 papers (58.2\%),
meaning that more than half the corpus does not assess prompting
techniques on any specific task type. These studies aggregate
performance across benchmarks spanning many different categories. This pattern
is partly an artifact of how surveys and taxonomy papers are classified, but it also reflects
a genuine tendency in the literature to report average scores across
MMLU or BIG-Bench without disaggregating results by individual task type.
(D5 Cross-model also counts 89 papers independently; the coincidence
in raw count reflects orthogonal classification dimensions, not duplicate coding.)

Among task-specific subgroups, reasoning tasks are the most studied
at 18 papers (11.8\%). Chain-of-thought prompting~\cite{wei2022chain}
was developed on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning benchmarks, and the
majority of early evaluations naturally adopted that framing. The combination of
strong techniques and well-established benchmarks in this area has created a
self-reinforcing concentration that persists across the literature.
Faithfulness-focused work in this subgroup -- Lanham et
al.~\cite{lanham2023faithfulness} and Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2025reasoningmodels} -- treats the reasoning chain
itself as the evaluation target rather than only the final answer.

Generation tasks appear in 12 papers (7.8\%), code tasks in 12
(7.8\%), and QA tasks in 8 (5.2\%). Clinical and multilingual
evaluation are present in two different senses in the corpus, and we
report both because confusing the two would distort the picture.
Counted by \emph{primary evaluation target}, one paper --
Sivarajkumar et al.~\cite{sivarajkumar2024empirical} -- centrally
adopts clinical NLP tasks (0.9\%), and two -- Vatsal and
Singh~\cite{vatsal2025multilingual} and Son et
al.~\cite{son2024mmeval} -- centrally adopt multilingual evaluation
(1.8\%). Counted by \emph{any-occurrence} (papers whose corpus or
evaluation includes at least one clinical or multilingual benchmark,
even if not the primary target), the corresponding shares are 3.9\%
and 2.6\% respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}); the additional
papers in those any-occurrence counts include MIRAGE / MedRAG, Nori
et al.\ on USMLE, Chang et al.\ on medical red-teaming, Kim et al.\
on medical hallucination, MGSM, and MM-Eval, none of which positions
its primary contribution in the clinical or multilingual domain. Two further papers, AgentBench~\cite{liu2023agentbench} and
AgentBoard~\cite{ma2024agentboard}, target agentic, multi-turn,
tool-use evaluation (1.8\%). Legal-domain evaluation is represented
by LegalBench~\cite{guha2023legalbench} and
LawBench~\cite{fei2023lawbench}; medical-domain evaluation by Nori et
al.'s GPT-4 USMLE study~\cite{nori2023gpt4medical}, the medical
red-teaming work of Chang et al.~\cite{chang2025redteamingmed}, and
the medical hallucination study of Kim et
al.~\cite{kim2025medicalhallu}. The corpus also includes
representative work in adjacent settings such as instruction-following
benchmarks (Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2023instruction}, IFEval),
sentiment-style reasoning (Qin et al.~\cite{qin2024chatgpt}), and the
multimodal evaluation benchmarks MME~\cite{fu2023mme} and
RH-Bench~\cite{liu2025multimodalhallu}. Foundational reasoning
benchmarks underpin many of these results: GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}
for grade-school math, GSM-Symbolic~\cite{mirzadeh2024gsmsymbolic} for
robustness to numerical perturbation, MGSM~\cite{shi2022mgsm} for
multilingual reasoning, and TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} for
truthfulness probing. Code-generation evaluation is represented by
SWE-bench~\cite{jimenez2023swebench},
EvalPlus~\cite{liu2023evalplus},
LiveCodeBench~\cite{jain2024livecodebench}, and Code
Llama~\cite{roziere2023codellama}.
Beyond these new entries, broader multilingual and clinical
coverage remains limited under our inclusion criteria. We are
deliberately careful with this point: an extensive body of
domain-specific prompting work
exists in the broader literature, including medical
QA~\cite{clinicalnlp2024}, clinical note
processing~\cite{medicalcot2025}, and multilingual
evaluation~\cite{vatsal2024survey}, and our screening filter
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) excluded papers that used prompting
as a tool without analyzing the prompting approach itself. The
finding is therefore best read as: the corpus of papers that propose
or evaluate \emph{prompt engineering techniques themselves} remains
strongly concentrated on multi-task benchmarks, with very limited
coverage of clinical or multilingual settings.

\subsection{Distribution across Metric Type (Dimension 3)}
\label{subsec:d3dist}

Task-specific metrics dominate at 102 papers (66.7\%). This is the
largest concentration observed across any single subcategory in the
entire taxonomy. Pass@$k$, exact match, F1, and accuracy scores are all
task-specific, and their prevalence closely mirrors the dominance of
benchmark evaluation in Dimension~1. The two dimensions are mutually
reinforcing: papers that adopt standard benchmarks naturally follow the
task-specific metrics those benchmarks prescribe.

LLM-judge metrics appear in 33 papers (21.6\%), essentially the same
set of papers that use LLM-as-judge evaluation in Dimension~1.
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023geval} showed that G-Eval with GPT-4
achieves stronger alignment with human judgment than BLEU or ROUGE on
summarization and dialogue tasks, and the field has shifted toward
LLM-based scoring for open-ended outputs in response.

Hybrid metric approaches appear in 5 papers (3.3\%). Model-based
metrics such as BERTScore appear in just 2 papers (1.3\%), with the
recent LLM-based NLG evaluation survey by Li et al.~\cite{li2024leveraging}
documenting how model-based scoring is gradually shifting toward
LLM-judge configurations.
Reference-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) serve as the \emph{primary}
measure in zero papers in the corpus. This is a notable reversal of
their historical dominance. It does not mean they are absent from the
literature; it means they are no longer considered sufficient as
standalone signals, and the field has moved beyond treating them as
the primary evaluation instrument. The documented gap between lexical
overlap and human judgment~\cite{repairing2022} made this shift
quite predictable.

\subsection{Distribution across Automation Level (Dimension 4)}
\label{subsec:d4dist}

Fully automated evaluation is the modal configuration. 124 of the 153
papers (81.0\%) run their assessments without any human involvement
at any stage of the evaluation loop. Hybrid evaluation, pairing automated
metrics with human validation on a subset, appears in 15 papers (9.8\%).
Human-in-loop evaluation, where human judgment is invoked selectively
on difficult cases, appears in 8 papers (5.2\%). Fully manual
evaluation accounts for just 6 papers (3.9\%).

This concentration at the automated end is consistent with the cost
and throughput pressures facing the field. It also has a specific
reproducibility implication: fully automated pipelines that depend
on proprietary LLM judges are stable within a run but not necessarily
across time, since the judge model can change between API versions.
Feng et al.~\cite{feng2025sage} showed that even the same model can
produce inconsistent judgments across runs for borderline cases. We
report this as a structural property of the configuration space
(addressed under Dimension~7 in Section~\ref{subsec:d7dist}) rather
than a moral claim about field practice.

\subsection{Distribution across Evaluation Scope (Dimension 5)}
\label{subsec:d5dist}

Cross-model evaluation is the most frequently used scope at 89 papers
(58.2\%). More than half the corpus explicitly tests whether findings
hold across multiple model families rather than a single architecture.
Studies such as Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}
and the BIG-Bench collaboration~\cite{srivastava2022bigbench}
apply the same prompting technique across several models
and report the variance in results that emerges.

Cross-domain evaluation appears in 30 papers (19.6\%), concentrated
primarily in the survey subgroup. Works such as
Sahoo et al.~\cite{sahoo2024systematic} and
Chang et al.~\cite{chang2023survey} span multiple task types by
design. Single-model evaluation appears in 18 papers (11.8\%).
Cross-dataset evaluation covers 8 papers (5.2\%). The remaining
papers either report cross-lingual evaluation as a primary
property (Vatsal and Singh~\cite{vatsal2025multilingual} and Son et
al.~\cite{son2024mmeval}, 2 papers) or are surveys for which D5 is
not directly applicable, with the underlying primary studies
already counted in the other categories. Percentages sum to less
than 100\% because a small number of multi-scope studies are
counted under their primary scope only.

The 18 single-model papers deserve particular attention. A prompting
technique evaluated on one model, typically GPT-4, at one point in
time on one benchmark produces a claim about performance in exactly
that configuration and no other.
Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023multi} documented this brittleness
directly, showing that single-prompt, single-model evaluations
routinely overstate technique performance relative to multi-prompt
baselines run on the same model. The implication is that a
meaningful share of claims in the PE literature are narrower in
scope than their phrasing in introductions and abstracts suggests.

\subsection{Three Recurring Evaluation Archetypes}
\label{subsec:archetypes}

The co-occurrence pattern resolves into three recurring archetypes
that together account for the bulk of the corpus. Each archetype is a
named, recurring configuration of dimensional choices, located at a
specific region of the validity-reliability space.

\textbf{Archetype A: Benchmark-Automation.}
Configuration: D1 = Benchmark, D3 = Task-specific, D4 = Automated,
D5 = Cross-model or Single-model, D6 = Single-prompt or
Not reported, D7 = Artifact-released, Contamination-checked rare.
Approximate share: $\sim$68\% of the corpus (all papers with
D1=Benchmark and D4=Automated; modal archetype for technique,
benchmark, and survey categories). Region of design space:
high reliability, narrow construct validity, reference-anchored.
Representative example: chain-of-thought
papers~\cite{wei2022chain, wang2023selfconsistency} and reasoning-
focused benchmark introductions~\cite{hendrycks2021measuring,
srivastava2022bigbench}. Strength: highly reproducible, comparable
across labs, well-matched to closed-form reasoning tasks. Weakness:
under-specifies the construct on open-ended generation, sensitive to
contamination, rarely reports prompt-paraphrase variance.

\textbf{Archetype B: Judge-Mediated.}
Configuration: D1 = LLM-as-judge, D3 = LLM-judge, D4 = Automated or
Hybrid, D5 = Cross-model, D6 = Multi-prompt aggregate or
Single-prompt, D7 = Judge-version-pinned where reported,
Contamination-checked rare. Approximate share: $\sim$24\% of the
corpus (all papers with D1=LLM-as-judge; concentrated in the
LLM-judge and metric-quality category).
Region of design space: judgment-anchored, broader construct
ambition than Archetype A, but reliability mediated entirely by the
underlying judge model. Representative example:
MT-Bench~\cite{zheng2023judging}, AlpacaEval~\cite{dubois2024alpacaeval},
and the LLM-judge analyses~\cite{gu2025llmjudge, feng2025sage,
alternativeannotator2025}. Strength: handles open-ended outputs
where Archetype A cannot. Weakness: judge bias and judge-version
drift are not yet managed by a shared calibration protocol.

\textbf{Archetype C: Expert-Anchored.}
Configuration: D1 = Human eval (sometimes plus LLM-judge as
adjunct), D3 = Hybrid or Model-based, D4 = Human-in-loop or Hybrid,
D5 = Single-model or Cross-model, D6 = Multi-prompt aggregate,
D7 = Artifact-released. Approximate share: $\sim$11\% of the
corpus (papers with D1=Human eval; Human-in-loop or Hybrid in D4).
Region of design space: judgment-anchored, highest construct
ambition, lowest within-run reliability. Representative example:
InstructGPT/RLHF~\cite{ouyang2022training} and the human-vs-LLM
calibration studies~\cite{llmvshuman2024, humansorllm2024,
canlllmsreplace2023}. Strength: takes construct validity seriously
in domains where reference-anchored evaluation is structurally
inadequate. Weakness: scales poorly, replicates expensively, and
inter-rater agreement is rarely strong.

The three archetypes are not exhaustive - the corpus also contains
hybrid combinations and small populations of ablation-driven and
user-study evaluations - but they account for the majority of papers
and they cover the three coherent regions of the validity-reliability
space. The taxonomy makes the archetype boundaries identifiable, the
tradeoffs each archetype pays explicit, and the design choices that
move a study from one archetype to another visible.

\subsection{Failure-Mode Catalog}
\label{subsec:failuremodes}

Beyond the distributional patterns, the seven-dimensional coding
surfaces specific cases in which a paper's evaluation configuration
does not support its contribution claim. We illustrate three such
failure modes drawn from the corpus, presented in compact form
below. The intent is not to single papers out, but to show what the
taxonomy makes visible that an unstructured reading would not.

\textit{Failure mode 1: validity-reliability mismatch on
open-ended tasks.} A paper proposing a new prompting technique for
text summarization or open-ended generation reports BLEU and ROUGE
on a fixed reference set (D1 = Benchmark, D3 = Reference-based or
Task-specific). The construct claim is ``improved summary quality'',
but the metric covers only n-gram overlap with one reference, and
the documented gap between lexical overlap and human judgment on
summarization~\cite{liu2023geval, repairing2022} is not addressed.
What the taxonomy makes visible: the configuration is
high-reliability and narrow-validity, while the contribution claim
requires the opposite balance. Corpus instances exhibiting this
pattern include the earlier reference-only generation evaluations
that motivated the G-Eval~\cite{liu2023geval} response and the
repair-evaluation argument of Gehrmann et
al.~\cite{repairing2022}; the gap is the gap they document.

\textit{Failure mode 2: judge-version drift undisclosed.} A paper in
the LLM-judge cluster reports score-based comparisons against a
proprietary judge model accessed via API, but does not pin the
judge's snapshot date or version. By the time of publication, the
judge model has had at least one silent revision. What the taxonomy
makes visible: D7 = Artifact-released without
Judge-version-pinned. Reproducibility in the within-run sense is
intact; reproducibility across time is not, because future attempts
to replicate cannot recover the same judge. The Judge-Sensitivity
analysis of Bellibaltu et al.~\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense} and the
SAGE work~\cite{feng2025sage} quantify exactly this drift; among the
LLM-judge papers in our corpus, only the 5 papers coded as D7 =
Judge-version-pinned (Table~\ref{tab:d7dist}, 3.3\% of the corpus
overall, 15.6\% of LLM-judge papers) document the judge snapshot in
a way that supports across-time reproducibility.

\textit{Failure mode 3: single-prompt cross-model claim.} A paper
demonstrates a prompting technique on five model families and
reports cross-model performance, but uses one prompt phrasing per
model. The technique's effect on the chosen phrasing is reported as
the technique's effect in general. What the taxonomy makes visible:
D5 = Cross-model with D6 = Single-prompt. Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2023multi} and Mizrahi et
al.~\cite{mizrahi2024state} have shown that single-prompt
evaluations overstate effects relative to multi-prompt baselines on
the same model; cross-model findings reported under D6 =
Single-prompt are therefore narrower than their phrasing suggests.
The chain-of-thought original evaluation~\cite{wei2022chain} and its
zero-shot variant~\cite{kojima2022large} are both coded D5 =
Cross-model with D6 = Single-prompt in our corpus
(Electronic Supplement, Table~B.1), making this failure mode endemic to the
modal Archetype A configuration.

These failure modes are not unusual; they appear at non-trivial
rates across the corpus, and the dimensional coding makes them
identifiable in a way that prose summaries do not.

\paragraph{Hidden assumptions surfaced by the dimensional view.}
Each failure mode is a place where an unstated assumption is
necessary to support the contribution claim. Naming the assumptions
explicitly is part of the
contribution of the taxonomy: each one is a candidate
\emph{reporting requirement} that a future evaluation standard
could codify.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Construct-equivalence assumption.} The headline claim
treats the benchmark metric as a stand-in for the deployment-context
property. This is defensible on closed-form tasks and
indefensible on open-ended ones, and the failure modes do not
indicate which case the paper is in. Surfaced by D2 + D3
combinations where a task-specific metric is applied to a
generation task scope.

\item \textit{Judge-stability assumption.} The headline claim
treats the LLM-judge score as time-invariant. Surfaced by D1 =
LLM-as-judge with D7 not pinning the judge version: future
re-runs cannot recover the same evaluator.

\item \textit{Paraphrase-invariance assumption.} The headline
claim treats the chosen prompt phrasing as representative of the
technique. Surfaced by D6 = Single-prompt combined with cross-model
scope.

\item \textit{Contamination-cleanliness assumption.} The headline
claim treats the test set as not seen during pretraining. Surfaced
by D7 lacking the contamination-checked property on a static
benchmark whose contents have plausibly leaked.

\item \textit{Category-representativeness assumption.} A
multi-task aggregate score is treated as evidence for the average
task type within the corpus. Surfaced by D2 = Multi-task with no
per-task disaggregation.
\end{itemize}

The five assumptions are not novel observations in isolation - each
appears in at least one prior critique of evaluation
practice~\cite{ni2025benchmarks, sclar2023formatting, feng2025sage,
wang2023multi}. What the taxonomy provides is a single coding scheme
that locates them in specific papers and quantifies how often each
assumption goes unaddressed across the corpus.

\section{Discussion and Future Directions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The per-dimension distributions and the archetype analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} are best read not as a series of
independent findings but as evidence of three underlying tensions
that structure prompt engineering evaluation. We name and analyze
each tension in turn, locate the empirical evidence for it in the
corpus, and identify the implications for evaluation design. A
future research agenda follows in
Section~\ref{subsec:agenda}, and limitations are stated in
Section~\ref{subsec:surveylimits}.

\subsection{Tension 1: Scalability vs.\ Construct Validity}
\label{subsec:tension_scalability}

The first tension is the most visible in the corpus. Archetype A
(Benchmark-Automation) maximizes within-run reliability and
throughput by anchoring evaluation to a fixed gold reference and a
deterministic scoring function. Archetype C (Expert-Anchored)
maximizes construct validity by anchoring evaluation to human
judgment, at the cost of throughput, replication speed, and
cross-paper comparability. The corpus is heavily weighted toward
Archetype A: the configuration share is consistent with the
distribution reported in Sections~\ref{subsec:d1dist}
and~\ref{subsec:d4dist}, and the relative scarcity of human
evaluation is consistent with Section~\ref{subsec:d1dist}.

The taxonomy clarifies what the tradeoff is and is not. It is not a
choice between rigorous and lazy evaluation - both archetypes are
internally rigorous in their own terms. It is a choice between
\textit{construct fidelity at the deployment context} and
\textit{measurement consistency across runs}. For tasks where the
construct is well-approximated by an exact-match score (arithmetic,
factual retrieval, code execution against unit tests), Archetype A
loses very little construct validity in exchange for very high
reliability. For tasks where the construct is not well-approximated
by exact-match (open-ended generation, clinical decision support,
multilingual fluency), Archetype A pays significant construct
validity for the same reliability, and the resulting score
under-determines what it claims to measure.

The implication is not that the field should adopt Archetype C
universally. It is that papers proposing prompting techniques
intended for deployment on tasks where construct fidelity is
methodologically central should be explicit that an Archetype-A
evaluation does
not directly support a deployment claim, and should provide either
Archetype B or C evidence (or a calibrated reference-free metric) to
close the gap.

\subsection{Tension 2: Reference-Anchored vs.\ Judgment-Anchored Evaluation}
\label{subsec:tension_reference}

The second tension organizes the boundary between Archetype A and
Archetype B. When no gold reference exists, evaluation must
delegate scoring to a judgment - human, expert panel, or LLM - and
the failure modes change accordingly. Reference-anchored evaluation
fails on under-determined references and contaminated test sets;
judgment-anchored evaluation fails on judge bias, judge-version
drift, and missing calibration.

The LLM-as-judge cluster (Archetype B) is the corpus's attempt to
preserve the throughput advantages of automation while moving from
reference-anchored to judgment-anchored scoring. The attempt is not
finished. The 32 papers in the cluster draw from at least six
distinct judge configurations -- different judge models, different
prompt templates, different scoring rubrics, different tie-handling
protocols - and few report a calibration experiment establishing
how closely the judge aligns with the target judgment on the
specific task under evaluation. The result is that LLM-judge scores
are reproducible within a paper but not directly comparable across
papers, which is a different and weaker reproducibility property
than the one Archetype A delivers.

Calderon et al.~\cite{alternativeannotator2025} have proposed a
statistical framework for deciding whether a given LLM judge is an
acceptable substitute for human annotators on a specific dataset.
Gehrmann et al.~\cite{repairing2022} have articulated broader NLG
reporting norms. Neither has been adopted at scale within the PE
evaluation literature. Closing this gap requires community adoption of existing standards
more than new technical development.

\subsection{Tension 3: Reliability Within a Run vs.\ Reliability Across Time}
\label{subsec:tension_reproducibility}

The third tension is the one D7 (Reproducibility and Contamination
Resistance) most directly captures. A pipeline can be highly
reliable in the within-run sense (the same input returns the same
score) and fragile in the across-time sense (the score depends on
infrastructure that drifts silently between API revisions, or on
test data that may have leaked into pretraining). Section~\ref{subsec:d7dist}
documents the asymmetry: artifact release is relatively common,
contamination checking and judge-version pinning are not.

This tension is structural rather than discretionary. As long as
benchmark evaluation depends on static test sets that grow more
likely to be contaminated as models train on more web-scale data,
and as long as LLM-judge evaluation depends on proprietary judge
models that the evaluation pipeline cannot pin or reproduce,
within-run reliability and across-time reliability will pull
against each other. Recent work on contamination-resistant dynamic
benchmarks~\cite{wildbench2024} and on judge-sensitivity
quantification~\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense, feng2025sage}
suggests that the tension is at least partially addressable with
infrastructure changes; what is missing is widespread adoption of
those changes within the PE evaluation literature itself.

\subsection{Open Problems by Tension}
\label{subsec:open_problems}

Each tension implies a specific class of open problem.

The scalability vs.\ validity tension implies a need for
\textit{calibrated reference-free metrics} for tasks where no gold
answer exists - metrics whose construct relationship to the target
property has been validated against an expert standard, and whose
calibration evidence is reported alongside their numerical scores.
Existing work on FActScore~\cite{factscore2023} and the alternative
annotator test~\cite{alternativeannotator2025} provides building
blocks; a domain-specific PE evaluation literature using these
building blocks does not yet exist in systematic form, particularly
for clinical and multilingual settings (with the corpus boundary
caveats from Section~\ref{subsec:d2dist}).

The reference vs.\ judgment tension implies a need for a
\textit{shared LLM-judge calibration standard}: a minimum bar of
disclosure (judge model and version, prompt template, calibration
outcome against a target judgment on the specific task) that
papers reporting LLM-judge scores would be expected to meet. The
PE community has the building blocks; what is missing is
adoption.

The within-run vs.\ across-time tension implies a need for
\textit{infrastructure-stable evaluation pipelines}: dynamic
contamination-resistant benchmarks, locally-pinned judge models
where licensing permits, or - at minimum - explicit reporting of
the snapshot date for any proprietary judge component. None of
these is a research breakthrough; each is an evaluation engineering
practice that the literature could converge on if reviewers
required it.

\subsection{Limitations of This Survey}
\label{subsec:surveylimits}

The findings of this survey are bounded by four typed limitations.
Each is named, scoped, and accompanied by a description of how it
constrains the claims a reader should draw from the corpus.

\subsubsection{Search-String Coverage Gaps}
\label{subsubsec:lim_search}
Search strings operate on controlled vocabulary in title, abstract,
and keyword fields, which means papers addressing the same concepts
under alternative or emerging terminology may have been missed.
Citation searching recovered 22 additional papers in the primary
round, and four subsequent targeted expansion rounds
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds})
recovered 73 more (20 + 11 + 35 + 7), but completeness cannot be
guaranteed. Because the expansion rounds used narrower topical
queries rather than full database sweeps, they introduce a
potential selection bias toward the targeted areas; we therefore
report distributional findings as descriptive of \emph{this corpus
under these inclusion and expansion criteria} rather than as
field-level estimates. Where the corpus shows a relative scarcity
(clinical and multilingual primary targets, in particular), we are
explicit
throughout this paper that the absence is a property of the corpus
under our inclusion criteria, not a claim about the field at large.

\subsubsection{Recency Bias Against 2025--2026 Work}
\label{subsubsec:lim_recency}
Papers published in 2025--2026 that have not yet accumulated
citations and were not published at a recognized venue fall outside
the retention criteria. The corpus may therefore underrepresent
recent methodological developments, particularly in the fast-moving
LLM-as-judge and dynamic-benchmark subfields. The expansion round
specifically targeted this gap by extending the search cutoff and
supplementing the primary search with domain-specific queries in
those areas, but the underlying recency bias of the citation
threshold remains. Additionally, two references in this survey
(\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense, benchmark2sq2026}) carry 2026
publication dates and are arXiv preprints that had not completed peer
review at the time of submission; their findings are treated as
preliminary and are not used as sole support for any claim that is
also supported by peer-reviewed evidence.

\subsubsection{Coding-Judgment Ambiguity at Boundary Cases}
\label{subsubsec:lim_coding}
D4 (automation level), D6 (robustness), and D7 (reproducibility)
required interpretation for papers that did not explicitly describe
their evaluation along the corresponding axis. The inter-rater
statistics in Section~\ref{subsec:coding_reliability} quantify the
resulting uncertainty per dimension, and the sensitivity analysis in
Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} establishes how much that
uncertainty propagates to the headline claims. We report what we
measured rather than presenting the coding as more rigorous than it
is.

\subsubsection{Corpus Scope Does Not Generalize to All PE Evaluation}
\label{subsubsec:lim_scope}
The findings apply only to papers matching the inclusion criteria
described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. In particular, the
corpus excludes domain-specific applied papers (e.g., clinical case
studies that do not generalize beyond a single narrow dataset) and
non-English papers. Patterns that appear structural in the corpus
may not hold in the excluded literature, and absence-of-evidence
findings (such as the low reporting rate of clinical or multilingual
PE evaluation) should not be read as evidence-of-absence at the
field level.

\subsubsection{Expansion-Round Selection Bias}
\label{subsubsec:lim_expansion}
The four targeted expansion rounds (Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds})
used narrower topical queries focused on specific underrepresented
areas (LLM-judge quality, agentic evaluation, foundation-model
reports, long-context tasks). This introduces directional selection
bias: papers addressing the targeted topics are over-represented
relative to their true prevalence in the field, while topics not
covered by any of the five rounds remain underrepresented. The
distributional findings in Section~\ref{sec:comparison} should
therefore not be extrapolated to topic areas absent from all five
search rounds, and cross-round comparisons of topic prevalence
should be interpreted with this bias in mind.

\subsection{A Future Research Agenda}
\label{subsec:agenda}

Seven research directions follow directly from the tensions identified
in Sections~\ref{subsec:tension_scalability}--\ref{subsec:tension_reproducibility}
and the open problems they imply. Each is named, scoped, and
mapped to the dimensions of the taxonomy it would affect.

\subsubsection{Domain-Specific Evaluation Frameworks for PE}
\label{subsubsec:fd_domain}
Rigorous evaluation protocols for clinical, legal, and multilingual
prompt engineering require reference-free metrics calibrated against
expert annotations, task-specific rubrics vetted by domain
professionals, and multilingual judge models that are not
English-dominant. Recent contamination-resistant dynamic
benchmarks~\cite{wildbench2024} provide one architectural template
for such frameworks. The clinical evaluation work of Sivarajkumar et
al.~\cite{sivarajkumar2024empirical}, the GPT-4 USMLE study of Nori
et al.~\cite{nori2023gpt4medical}, the medical red-teaming of Chang
et al.~\cite{chang2025redteamingmed}, and the medical hallucination
study of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025medicalhallu} occupy this direction
in our corpus. Legal-domain evaluation is anchored by
LegalBench~\cite{guha2023legalbench} and
LawBench~\cite{fei2023lawbench}. Retrieval-augmented evaluation for
domain-specific deployment is operationalized by
MedRAG~\cite{xiong2024medrag}, ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2023ares}, and
RGB~\cite{chen2023rgb}; the broader RAG evaluation methodology is
surveyed by Yu et al.~\cite{yu2024ragsurvey} and Zhao et
al.~\cite{zhao2024aigcrag}.

\subsubsection{A Shared LLM-Judge Calibration Standard}
\label{subsubsec:fd_calibration}
Any study reporting LLM-as-judge scores should conduct a calibration
experiment against a held-out human-annotated sample on the specific
task, and disclose the judge model identity, prompt template, and
calibration outcome alongside its primary results. The alternative
annotator test~\cite{alternativeannotator2025} offers a principled
statistical foundation that the PE community could adopt as a
minimum bar. Verga et al.~\cite{verga2024replacing} (PoLL) and the
ScaleEval framework of Chern et al.~\cite{chern2024scaleeval}
provide concrete architectural patterns. The broader calibration
literature is consolidated in the survey of Geng et
al.~\cite{geng2023calibration} and the empirical confidence-elicitation
study of Xiong et al.~\cite{xiong2023uncertainty}; the
calibration-gap finding of Steyvers et
al.~\cite{steyvers2024calibgap} in \emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}
provides direct evidence that users systematically overestimate LLM
confidence even when models are themselves well-calibrated, which
strengthens the case for required disclosure of judge calibration
metadata.

\subsubsection{Cross-Model, Cross-Dataset, and Cross-Paraphrase Scope as a Minimum Bar}
\label{subsubsec:fd_scope}
Single-model, single-prompt results should be treated as exploratory
and hypothesis-generating. Papers proposing new prompting techniques
should be expected to demonstrate consistent performance across at
least two model families, at least two benchmarks within the target
task type, and at least two paraphrases of the prompt before
advancing generalizability claims~\cite{wang2023multi,
sclar2023formatting, promptsensitivity2025, butterfly2024}.

\subsubsection{Robustness as a First-Class Evaluation Dimension}
\label{subsubsec:fd_robustness}
The sensitivity literature has established that paraphrase-induced
variance is often large enough to reverse study conclusions, and the
Judge Sensitivity Score (JSS) work of Bellibatlu et
al.~\cite{bellibatlu2026judgesense} extends this concern to the
evaluation infrastructure itself. JSS is defined as the share of
hand-validated paraphrase pairs on which a judge returns the same
verdict, with companion metrics of flip rate, Cohen's $\kappa$, and
bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals; the released benchmark contains
500 paraphrase pairs across four evaluation tasks (factuality,
coherence, relevance, preference) and is reported for 13 judge
models. The empirical result that model scale fails to predict
consistency on this benchmark is the kind of finding the field
currently lacks the conventions to report. Reporting sensitivity
bounds alongside point estimates, that is the distinction between
D6 = Single-prompt and D6 = Sensitivity-bounded in our taxonomy, is
a practical change that does not require new theory. The PromptEval
framework of Polo et al.~\cite{polo2024promptseval} is a companion
example of how such sensitivity-bounded evaluation can be made
budget-efficient for the task-solver side of the same problem.

\subsubsection{Multimodal and Agentic PE Evaluation Frameworks}
\label{subsubsec:fd_multimodal}
\label{subsec:multimodal}
Evaluation frameworks for multimodal prompting (image + text, audio
+ text) and for multi-step agentic pipelines do not map cleanly
onto any of the three archetypes identified in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison}. D2 (Task Scope) has been extended in
this work to include explicit Agentic and Multimodal subcategories
(Section~\ref{subsec:d2}), but the D1 / D3 / D4 configurations that
dominate Archetype~A were designed for single-turn, text-only
settings and do not transfer cleanly. Examples in the corpus include
the MME multimodal benchmark of Fu et al.~\cite{fu2023mme} and the
$\tau$-bench tool-agent benchmark of Yao
et al.~\cite{yao2024taubench}. As agentic systems become primary
PE deployment contexts, evaluation frameworks that address multi-step
task completion, tool use, and inter-agent consistency will need
companion D1, D3, and D6 conventions; we view this as a near-term
direction rather than a closed problem.

\subsubsection{Standardized Evaluation Reporting Templates}
\label{subsubsec:fd_reporting}
Even without changing evaluation methods, a community-endorsed
minimum-bar reporting template, analogous to the CONSORT checklist
in clinical trials~\cite{raji2021ai} or the NLG evaluation reporting
norms of Gehrmann et al.~\cite{repairing2022}, would make existing
results comparable without requiring methodological consensus. The
evaluation design checklist in the Electronic Supplement (Appendix~A) is one
version of such a template, designed to surface the seven taxonomy
decisions before evaluation begins. The direction for the field is
for a venue-endorsed version to be adopted at major NLP and AI
conferences as a submission requirement alongside reporting of
hardware, model version, and random seeds.

\subsubsection{Safety, Bias, and Adversarial Robustness Evaluation}
\label{subsubsec:fd_safety}
Evaluation frameworks for prompt engineering have largely treated
safety and bias as concerns adjacent to the core task-completion
question rather than as first-class evaluation dimensions. The
jailbreak literature -- Wei et al.~\cite{wei2023jailbroken} on safety
failure modes, GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer},
RED-EVAL~\cite{bhardwaj2023redeval}, Huang et al.\ on catastrophic
jailbreak via generation-strategy attacks~\cite{huang2023catastrophic},
the Constitutional Classifiers defense of Sharma et
al.~\cite{sharma2025constitutional}, and the Yi et al.\ attack-defense
survey~\cite{yi2024jailbreaksurvey} -- consistently shows that
deployed LLMs remain vulnerable to adversarial prompting even after
extensive safety training. The bias and fairness literature, surveyed
by Gallegos et al.~\cite{gallegos2023bias} and operationalized by
ROBBIE~\cite{esiobu2023robbie}, makes a parallel argument for
demographic robustness. A PE evaluation framework that omits these
dimensions over-states the deployment-readiness of any technique it
endorses. The direction for the field is for evaluation reports of
new prompting techniques to include at minimum a structured
adversarial-robustness probe and a demographic-bias measurement
alongside the main accuracy claims.

\subsection{Using This Taxonomy in Citing Work}
\label{subsec:using}

The taxonomy is designed to be a shared vocabulary, which means it is
most useful when citing work can reuse the D1--D7 labels directly
rather than re-stating evaluation-design choices in prose. We
recommend three concrete uses.

\textit{Methodology disclosure.} Papers proposing or evaluating a
prompting technique can embed the seven-tuple in a single line of
the methodology section, for example: ``Our evaluation
configuration is D1\,=\,Benchmark, D2\,=\,Reasoning,
D3\,=\,Task-specific, D4\,=\,Automated, D5\,=\,Cross-model,
D6\,=\,Multi-prompt aggregate, D7\,=\,Artifact-released, following
the taxonomy of [this paper].'' This compact format makes the
evaluation choices auditable and immediately comparable across
studies.

\textit{Comparative claims.} Reviews, meta-analyses, and surveys that
compare prompting techniques can use the taxonomy as the comparison
spine. The 153-paper companion dataset (planned for release at
acceptance to the URL in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}; see Data
Availability statement) will provide machine-readable codings that
downstream syntheses can reuse rather than re-coding the same
papers.

\textit{Evaluation design.} Researchers planning a new evaluation can
use the seven-dimensional checklist in
the Electronic Supplement (Appendix~A) as a preregistration template,
forcing each dimension choice to be made explicitly before data
collection begins rather than implicitly as a byproduct of
benchmark selection.

A canonical citation form for the taxonomy is:
``We adopt the seven-dimensional prompt-engineering evaluation
taxonomy (D1 evaluation method, D2 task scope, D3 metric type, D4
automation level, D5 evaluation scope, D6 robustness, D7
reproducibility) of [this paper] to position our evaluation
relative to the prior literature.''

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Prompt engineering evaluation has been treated as a procedural
choice rather than a measurement problem. The procedural framing
makes it difficult to compare evaluation results across studies and
to reason about which choices are methodologically central to a
given contribution claim. This survey reframes the practice as a
measurement problem and develops the conceptual structure that
follows from doing so.

Two underlying tensions from measurement theory organize the
configuration space: construct validity vs.\ measurement reliability,
and reference-anchored vs.\ judgment-anchored evaluation. Seven
dimensions -- evaluation method, task scope, metric type, automation
level, evaluation scope, robustness, and reproducibility and
contamination resistance -- give those tensions operational form.
Applied to a PRISMA 2020 systematic review of 153 papers
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, Figure~\ref{fig:prisma},
Table~\ref{tab:expansion_rounds}), the taxonomy produces three
findings beyond per-dimension frequency counts: the corpus resolves into three recurring evaluation
\textit{archetypes} occupying loosely defined regions of the
validity-reliability space; co-occurrence patterns make the
boundaries between archetypes legible; and a catalog of failure
modes shows where current configurations do not support the
contribution claims they accompany.

Three open problems follow directly. Tasks without ground-truth
answers still lack calibrated reference-free metrics. The LLM-as-judge
cluster has no shared calibration standard, so scores produced by
different judge configurations are not comparable across labs.
Evaluation pipelines that depend on hosted models are not
infrastructure-stable, since the judge can change silently between
API versions. All three are addressable with existing technical
components; what they require is adoption, not new theory.

The taxonomy is not a complete solution. It does not settle
disagreements about which evaluation method is optimal for a given
task, and it cannot supply the domain expertise needed to build
rigorous protocols in specialized settings. What it does is name
the tradeoffs that currently go unnamed, locate them in the
literature, and provide a vocabulary in which methodological
standardization is at least describable. The distance from
describable to standardized is now a coordination problem.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}
This work is a meta-level systematic review conducted entirely over
publicly available published and preprint papers. No human subjects
research was conducted, and no personally identifiable information
was collected or processed. All cited work is attributed accurately
to the original authors with complete bibliographic records. The
survey does not involve experiments on human participants, animal
subjects, or sensitive data; accordingly, no IRB review was
required. Potential dual-use concerns (e.g., evaluation frameworks
that could be adapted to adversarial prompt engineering) are
mitigated by the descriptive and analytical nature of the survey,
which characterizes existing evaluation methodology rather than
introducing new attack capabilities.
